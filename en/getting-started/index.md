## 2. I am a student of BBA / Polytechnic / Medicine. I have no programming experience. How do I get started?

1. Machine learning is for everyone. It is entirely possible to begin using common sense. In fact, people outside the computer science field are using it the most: Business Administration, Linguistics, Healthcare, Law, Human Resources, and many more. If someone wants to build English-to-Bangla translation or Text-to-Speech using machine learning, who will do it? A linguist. Who will determine which employees might leave your company in the coming months using machine learning? An HR professional.

2. Treat machine learning as a general tool, much like basic arithmetic. This tool is now being utilized across almost every discipline. The earlier you learn it, the further ahead you will be in your own field. One thing is evident: those without a computer science background often feel deeply intimidated by it. In reality, this subject is relevant and accessible to people from all academic backgrounds.

3. Machine learning does not involve the kind of rigid traditional programming that people imagine. What is there can easily be understood through common sense and basic logic. Furthermore, useful code snippets for various tasks are readily available on platforms like Stack Overflow. Once again: do not be afraid at all!

## 12. How do I know if a problem can be solved with machine learning?

Answer: Typically, machine learning answers stem from historical data in our possession. Sometimes the answer is a binary yes or no — a classification problem. Other times it is a continuous numerical output, such as: how many months until our return on investment materializes, or when might a customer churn? We call these 'regression' problems.

Alternatively, we might segment customers into cluster groups based on their behavioral patterns to provision new services. This represents an 'unsupervised' learning problem. [One-breath reading link](https://raqueeb.gitbook.io/scikit-learn/tools/supervised).

## 13.1 Do I need to know programming, statistics, and math at the beginning?

Short answer: No. But if you never step onto the road, how will you learn the path? My philosophy is simple: step onto the road. If we never lose our way, how will we know whether we are on the right track? Over the past five years of answering thousands of inbox questions, one pattern has stood out sharply: a large percentage of people ask the exact same question over a prolonged period. It is as if the person who asked the question is still asking it months later. This indicates they are caught in a state of paralysis and confusion. My unequivocal answer is: no, you do not need them at the very start. A major part of data science is 'domain knowledge'. In healthcare and agriculture, where data science is making massive inroads, did practitioners spend years mastering pure programming, statistics, and mathematics before starting?

Although problem solving and programming are related, anyone who understands how a human solves a problem can begin machine learning. What you need at the beginning is an understanding of how to represent data in the context of the problem. And the answer depends on whom you ask: programmers will tell you one thing, while people who work with real-world data have an entirely different perspective. Truthfully, there is ongoing debate about how much programming skill is genuinely required in the machine learning ecosystem. And why wouldn't there be? Machine learning was born precisely to break free from traditional rule-based programming.

What happens in conventional programming? Suppose you are programming to automate a process. You tell a robot to travel from point A to point B. That means you must specify every detail of the route: walk 10 steps forward at 90 degrees, then 20 steps to the right, then 15 steps at 170 degrees, and you will reach point B. If it encounters an obstacle anywhere along the way, it halts with an exit code. Now if you tell it to go to point C, all previous instructions are invalid.

What does machine learning do instead?

We show the machine many different routes for going from one point to another. When it runs through thousands of routes, it discovers a single underlying pattern: as it moves from the starting point toward the destination, the remaining distance decreases. Therefore, it chooses the path that minimizes distance. Afterwards, we test it on an unfamiliar road to see whether it can navigate successfully. Of course it can — because it understands the pattern. It is the difference between giving someone a fish and teaching them how to fish, so they never depend on others.

Can this be understood without programming? Absolutely. There is no need to worry about what critics say right now. I say: no, you do not need programming at the very beginning. What you need is a tool like Microsoft Excel, or the 'R' package — which takes just one day to learn. I have written a dedicated post on this.

Many advise mastering complete mathematics and programming before setting out on this journey. That is entirely mistaken. In reality, by the time someone finishes trying to learn everything exhaustively, their enthusiasm for the core subject has evaporated. Everyone agrees that learning what you need, when you need it, is the most effective approach. Some will tell you to learn the basics first before touching ML. It is unfair to claim you cannot succeed without doing that first. I myself only delved into higher-degree polynomials (the deep underlying math) when trying to understand a specific problem — never beforehand. I dug in, asked myself how much understanding was necessary to get the job done, and stopped there.

When you step onto the road, you learn as you move forward. At nearly fifty years old, I am continually learning new things; I certainly do not feel the need to ask anyone whether I should learn this or that. For instance, when list operations are needed in machine learning, and a list-of-lists operation is required alongside the basics, I learn the `zip` operation under the pressure of necessity. If you want to prepare, simply understanding variables, lists, arrays, loops, if-else statements, and dictionaries is more than enough. If you know how to interact with the operating system — reading, writing, and saving files — half the battle is already won!

Begin machine learning: wonderful tools exist that allow you to explore data without knowing programming — such as NumPy and pandas. You do not need deep programming knowledge. But as you dig deeper, you will notice where these tools reach their limits. A quick Google search will reveal that writing a simple loop or defining a helper function avoids having to write the same code repeatedly.

Hit the road. Once you learn to navigate by getting lost, nobody will be able to sell you hollow theories. Learning is an experience; I cannot explain riding a bicycle through a thousand lectures. You will fall many times; nobody learns without scraping their knees. Throw yourself into the challenge. If you cannot learn it, perhaps this path is not for you. Machine learning exists precisely so we do not have to write manual code. We can talk about that another day.

## 24. What is the current job market situation?

Management teams and founders of several e-commerce platforms and service industries frequently ask me to help them find "good" data scientists. However, I have been able to refer very few people — because our data scientists and industry use-cases do not yet align at that level of execution. This is not their fault; it is a market gap. In a nascent industry, there is a divergence between company expectations and candidate readiness. This gap will not last forever.

Our data industry is maturing; however, due to a shortage of well-labeled enterprise data, outcomes take time to materialize. On a promising note, professionals from other industries who consult with me on applying ML to their sector's specific problems learn remarkably well. Ultimately, those who can define their problem clearly discover solutions the fastest.

**Summary:** Wherever you are, in whichever industry you work, try solving a real problem using machine learning. Challenge yourself. You will learn at lightning speed. Then you will be well prepared to transition into companies that focus purely on data science.

## 25. There are hardly any jobs in Bangladesh — what should I do?

Many beginners wonder: what is the point of learning this? Will I land a job? There are barely any jobs in the Bangladeshi market! So why should we learn machine learning? The few jobs that exist demand extensive experience, which has become a major source of frustration. I personally hear from many who feel disheartened. **What is your advice for them?**

An excellent observation. Thank you. You are entirely correct: everyone naturally wants a job after acquiring a skill. That is completely expected. Many ask me this, and I do not view it as unusual.

I tell everyone: over the course of our lives, we must develop multiple skillsets. Gone are the days when you could enter a single job and stay there for life. The times are far more demanding. I hold over 70 certificates across diverse disciplines. I realize now that certificates and degrees hold little weight when an employer sees that a candidate cannot deliver what is needed.

A company hires someone to solve a specific problem. Degrees and certificates serve merely as preliminary screening filters when the market is flooded with applicants. However, companies increasingly bypass rigid degree requirements when assessing whether someone can solve the task at hand.

In my own career, I learned whatever was needed, whenever it was needed. Over 28 years of professional life, I have reskilled myself 7 times. Talented colleagues and programmers I work with continuously update their skillsets to stay ahead of the game. Nobody wants to become sidelined or redundant. Retirement is a different matter.

## 26. What is your career advice in a nutshell?

1. When someone treats mastering a skillset as a vital personal mission, the outcome is extraordinary. Put everything into mastering the specific skill, not merely hunting for a job. Learn the necessary tools and stay updated. When you do, employers look at your GitHub profile rather than social media profiles. Companies will speak with you directly about their specific challenges. If you can outline solutions to their problems, landing the role becomes straightforward. Active participation and helping others in online forums also opens doors to unexpected opportunities.

2. In past generations, people could get by with a narrow set of skills throughout their careers. That no longer works. You must acquire multiple skillsets over a lifetime. Along the way, unlearning outdated methods and embracing new skillsets — what we call reskilling — is mandatory.

Finally, the market is emerging. Many people I know are receiving data science offers from Europe and Japan. The market is developing. Think globally, not just locally. While working in your current role, or from wherever you are right now, start learning machine learning. "Who knows when lightning could strike?"
