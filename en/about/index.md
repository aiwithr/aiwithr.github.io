## Why Am I Writing So Many Books?

A major portion of my life has been spent plotting data — drawing actionable wisdom out of data. I have introduced, and continue to integrate, extensive data into national public policy-making processes. That journey has gifted me extraordinary firsthand experiences with data. I have seen with my own eyes how data can be channeled into public policy for the greater human good.

Enlightened economies identified data as a 'strategic asset' long ago to maintain their competitive edge. A strategic asset is that vital 'soft power' required to attain true excellence in any endeavor. While we possess abundant raw data, our human capital has not been developed to systematically extract 'wisdom' or deep 'insights' from that data. This website is the foundational stepping stone toward bridging that gap. The elementary stage of engineering is learning how to get from point A to point B. My teaching philosophy is slightly different: I want you to lose your way along that road and discover point C instead. That is where my true fulfillment lies.

My passion for writing began in early childhood, effortlessly writing pages after pages. With the advent of the internet in Bangladesh, I transitioned into digital media, which formalised through my [WordPress blog](https://raqueeb.wordpress.com/) starting in 2001. Additional profiles and updates are available on [LinkedIn](https://www.linkedin.com/in/raqueeb/) and [Facebook](https://www.facebook.com/raqueeb).

## Roaming the World

The journey began nearly a decade ago. A career defined by constant movement — here today, there tomorrow. Eventually, I was posted as a UN Peacekeeper in the Democratic Republic of the Congo. It was life on the move. Excluding hours of sleep, almost the entire day was spent on the road. Navigating your Area of Responsibility (AOR) inch by inch was critical; human lives hung in the balance. The entire operational territory had to be at your fingertips through constant vigilance. Between patrols on foot and by vehicle, my odometer easily crossed hundreds of thousands of miles.

## Plotting Thoughts

The moment you hit the road, a thousand thoughts swarm through your mind — pulling in every direction without clear shape. Returning to base camp every few days, I would sit before the UN's turtle-paced satellite internet connection — our sole backward linkage to modern civilization. Eventually, I began plotting those thoughts. They had to be channelized somewhere. Each thought became a dot. The profound concept of 'connecting the dots' became clear to me much later. They say you rarely realize it before turning forty.

## The World's Largest Telecommunications Training Facility

What came earlier was even more intriguing. Just before departing for the Congo, I had returned from an extended posting at the United States Signal School in Georgia. Covering nearly ninety square miles, it is the largest telecommunication training installation in the world. The location itself was nestled in rural Georgia; after dusk, it was quiet enough to remind you of lantern-lit nights. Returning from school with time on my hands in the evening, I turned my attention to cooking — how long can one survive on fast food alone? I would pick up ingredients from the PX and conduct wild culinary 'tests and trials' at home. What was the risk? I was my own guinea pig! I finally dialed it back after setting off the smoke detector's water sprinkler several times. But I did not give up entirely; I simply slowed down and redirected my focus toward a new horizon.

## Inspiration to Write: A Million Words

There was a massive library. I would check out books and materials with generous lending limits. Historical periodicals and rare volumes were preserved on microfiche. If a newly published book was not yet in circulation, the librarian would procure it and hold it at checkout for me. Tracking Amazon bestseller lists became second nature during that period. To my amazement, I noticed that bestsellers were frequently written by ordinary citizens — everyday practitioners rather than professional authors. That gave me immense courage. Who decreed that whatever I write must immediately find a mass audience? Write to nourish the soul. Since 1992, I have often recalled Seth Godin's rallying cry: *Ship, buddy! Ship!* I have written hundreds of thousands of words, and today, those disparate ideas connect effortlessly.

## Standing on the Shoulders of Giants

It turned out that those bestselling authors included stock exchange leaders, CIA directors, investment bankers, public administrators, Navy SEALs, football coaches, and gymnasts. They wrote directly from decades of frontline experience, distilling failure into blueprints for success. This experiential wisdom guided avid readers in making sound life and professional decisions. Writers addressed controversial realities openly — seasoned professionals like Rachel Moran and others chronicled their journeys across virtually every domain. Failures were examined transparently, and through those shared mistakes, entire societies evolved. Cultures that embrace constructive critique rise rapidly. Founding fathers penned their visions too; the architect of modern Singapore documented his insights across several books. You have surely read Nelson Mandela's autobiography: how he dissolved the bitterness of twenty-seven years of unjust imprisonment in a single transcendent moment would have remained unimaginable without reading his personal account. Reading extensively allows us to separate the profound from the superficial, retaining the good and letting go of the rest as time evolves — learning collaboratively rather than tearing each other down.

## Reusable Knowledge

Knowledge is inherently reusable. It took Great Britain nearly two centuries from the dawn of the Industrial Revolution simply to learn and refine its industrial processes. The United States learned from Britain by dispatching its own observers, who returned by ship with notebooks packed with firsthand industrial practices. Applying that knowledge, America rivaled British supremacy in a fraction of the time, cutting the technological lead time by nearly a century. Because the United States nurtured that rigorous culture of learning, it continues to lead. Despite numerous headwinds, the European Union endures through innovation; facing domestic demographic constraints, they focus on securing global markets. In our era, conflicts are no longer fought through warfare; the true contest is economic dominance — creating sustainable outbound cash flows without shedding a drop of blood!

Asian economies demonstrated even greater strategic foresight. Over the past three decades, they absorbed core practices from Europe and North America and deployed them domestically, now competing on equal terms with the best. Hong Kong, South Korea, and Singapore accelerated by capitalizing on the hard-learned lessons of others. Once you understand what succeeded in the West and what failed, you combine that knowledge with local conditions. I describe this as knowledge diffusion: synthesizing multifaceted streams of insight. In a developing nation, public officials are afforded overseas exposures to bring home valuable expertise. When corporate institutions practice frugality, meetings shift to video conferences. The nation's fundamental expectation is that acquired knowledge must directly propel domestic development. Once foundational knowledge is gained, the internet serves as the ultimate vehicle for self-directed learning. That is why democratizing internet access matters so deeply: knowledge is abundant all around us; our singular imperative is execution.

## 'Best Practices', Applied Locally

Imagine acquiring insight A from the United States, B from Singapore, and C from Vietnam. Bangladesh can evaluate these against domestic statistical indicators and adopt whatever fits our needs at any given moment. This is what many term 'best practices' — methodologies that have been rigorously tested and validated across multiple jurisdictions. Much like proven examination solution guides, following verified practices drastically minimizes failure rates. Harmonizing international best practices with local contextual realities is where true craftsmanship and mastery reside. For seven continuous years, I dedicated myself to building operational templates to expand internet infrastructure nationwide.

## Knowledge Leapfrogging

Remember Sir Isaac Newton's immortal declaration: *"If I have seen further, it is by standing on the shoulders of giants."* Modern development across the developed world rests entirely on this principle. Today, we know what a transistor is and how millions of them are etched into a single microchip. Rather than reinventing the transistor from scratch, the real question is: what breakthrough solutions can we build using a multi-million-transistor chip? By mastering the diffusion of existing knowledge and applying it to new innovations, emerging economies can leapfrog traditional development cycles.

## Learning from Data

I joined BTRC with specialized background in military spectrum management. At the time, I contributed occasional technology columns to national dailies. Resistance surfaced immediately. Whenever I wrote about broadband or internet expansion, many shook their heads in skepticism:

*"The concept is noble, but the roadblock is systemic. It simply cannot be realized in our country."*

I listened in astonishment. Progress was throttled by outdated policy constraints — in other words, we were ensnared in our own regulatory net. Without understanding the operational documentation, societies lag behind. The foundational benefit of connectivity is human empowerment: it originates with economic independence, while knowledge guarantees our rightful entitlements. Later, while embedded in an agency, I worked directly with large-scale social media data. What the world witnesses today through universal connectivity is merely the visible tip of an iceberg. The Arab Spring was simply an early symptom. The world is evolving rapidly, and we must evolve alongside it for the better — armed with essential tools like the internet.

## Getting Books Out of My Head

Writing these books is an act of civic duty. It was my way of releasing the intense mental buildup of seven intense years at BTRC. Because carrying a pocket notebook was mandatory with military uniform, note-taking became second nature. Jim Rohn's timeless reminder — *"Never trust your memory"* — kept me disciplined. In our digital age, Google Keep and Evernote have taken over. Elephant memory ensures nothing falls by the wayside. Wherever I traveled across national and international forums, I gathered data and penned observations the moment a window opened. My notebooks, Evernote files, and Keep voice memos overflowed. In my mind, these ideas were interconnected like programming pointers. Before memory could fade, writing books was the only remedy.

## Tenacious Research Outcomes

Another personal trait has driven me since childhood: once I undertake a project, I cannot rest until I see it through to completion. It can be exhausting. I used to think this intensity was unique to me, but exploring the world disproved that notion. Global industries operate on the exact same tenacity: research initiatives persist through relentless determination until breakthrough is achieved. Consider Henry Ford's legendary pursuit of the cast V-8 engine block. This persistence defines defense engineering, aerospace, NASA, and advanced pharmaceutical research. The Airbus A380 double-decker superjumbo did not materialize overnight: research initiated in 1988 bore fruit in April 2005 when the aircraft took flight. Even then, more than two years of exhaustive safety certifications followed before Singapore Airlines operated the first commercial flight. Pure determination: it *had* to fly, so it flew. Key stakeholders held firm: *"We cannot fund fuel consumption for ten flights; engineer fuel efficiency into one flight, or exit the business."* Boeing's 787 Dreamliner was similarly brought to life by a band of resolute engineers. Providence has endowed humanity with profound intellect; the execution rests entirely with us.

## "I Didn't Fail a Thousand Times"

The birth of the modern internet — which has reshaped our civilization — followed this identical paradigm. Driven to move past fragile and expensive circuit-switched communications, the Defense Advanced Research Projects Agency (DARPA) channeled immense funding and patience across multiple research universities. Initiatives failed thousands of times, yet they never gave up. The result was ARPANET, the direct ancestor of today's internet. When Thomas Edison laboured through over a thousand prototypes to develop the incandescent light bulb, a journalist questioned how it felt to fail a thousand times. Edison famously replied: *"I didn't fail a thousand times; the light bulb was an invention with a thousand steps."*

## Specialization: Million-Dollar Consulting

In the telecommunications sector, superficial operational work yields little value; regulatory oversight demands the same depth. Deep domain specialization is essential to decipher market dynamics. You must penetrate beneath the surface — this is inherently the domain of quantitative economists. Without the ability to anticipate future trajectories, surviving in telecom is exceptionally challenging. Even seasoned industry veterans within telecom operators occasionally struggle to attain that specialized depth. That is precisely where specialized global telecommunication consulting firms step in: their primary role is year-round R&D across multiple operators worldwide. Consequently, their advisory solutions command multi-million dollar fees. And deservedly so: that caliber of R&D requires monumental dedication. Over the years, I have scrutinized hundreds of reports from these elite consulting firms. They read almost like palmistry: they literally map out future industry landscapes. When you assign a goal — such as generating ten million jobs — quantitative consulting formulates the practical, actionable blueprint. Working hands-on with big data opened my eyes to this reality.

## The Competence to Evaluate Consulting Reports

However, not all consulting engagements are equal. If you ask me: rather than finding fault with consulting firms, one must examine the client's competence to evaluate the deliverables. If technical competence is lacking on the receiving end, the outcome will inevitably falter. When purchasing a multi-million-dollar report without the internal capacity to interpret it, blaming the consultant is futile. Developing nations frequently engage costly consultancies funded by donor agencies, yet lack the technical depth to digest and implement the findings. When an organization possesses the internal rigor to absorb an advisory report as its own, its value multiplies tenfold. Over my career, I thoroughly evaluated several such landmark studies — each possessing the depth of post-doctoral research.

## Understanding Our Systemic 'Fault Lines'

Through my regulatory and policy work, I collaborated with world-renowned telecommunication consulting firms, alongside independent advisors from the International Telecommunication Union (ITU) and the Commonwealth Telecommunications Organisation (CTO). I absorbed their methodologies and studied thousands of high-value technical reports commissioned globally. I engaged deeply with prestigious firms including Ovum, Accenture, KPMG, PwC, NERA, and Analysys Mason. Furthermore, during national regulatory projects, I collaborated directly with the American firm Telecommunications Management Group, Inc. (TMG). Deciphering their strategic frameworks initially took considerable effort; these are billion-dollar advisory institutions. I forged lasting professional relationships with many of their experts, who walked me through Bangladesh's underlying systemic fault lines.

## How Management Consulting Firms Operate

Over time, I unlocked the operational philosophy of global management consulting firms. Having executed assignments worldwide, they possess an intimate understanding of where operational bottlenecks exist — and because charting recovery pathways is their sole business, their execution is exceptionally thorough. In developing nations, when multi-million-dollar consultancies fail to translate into tangible progress, the blame cannot be placed solely on external firms. If institutional recipients fail to evaluate deliverables down to the last detail, sustainable change remains elusive. For developing countries to accelerate growth, specialized advisory is essential, but it requires local leaders capable of scrutinizing every insight. When critical knowledge is overlooked during ceremonial foreign study tours, the nation squanders both intellect and long-term wisdom.

## A Business Plan for Governing a Nation

In many respects, governance advisory mirrors purchasing an enterprise business plan: an expert formulates the strategy, but one cannot invest public funds blindly without understanding its mechanics. When taxpayers' money is at stake, accountability is paramount. That is why developed nations consistently optimize the administrative footprint of government, pursuing strategic public-private partnerships (PPP). Anyone who has traveled through Thailand is struck by its world-class mega-infrastructure — yet the majority was executed under the PPP model. Governments worldwide do not maintain limitless treasuries. Under PPP, the private sector supplies capital while the government provides regulatory certainty and statutory frameworks. It creates a win-win: public treasuries are preserved, infrastructure is delivered, and massive employment is generated. Our domestic PPP framework has yet to achieve that level of efficacy.

## Infrastructure Funds

In advanced economies, citizens do not depend solely on state budgets for capital-intensive public works because PPP mechanisms bridge the gap. A nation's development index is vividly reflected in its urban mass transit: how efficiently citizens can traverse the metropolitan grid within predictable timeframes. In Dhaka, urban mobility compares unfavorably even with many African capitals. Yet with business-enabling PPP frameworks, private asset management consortiums could submit unsolicited infrastructure proposals to the state, creating dedicated 'Infrastructure Funds'. The metropolitan area could be partitioned into four or five quadrants, with concession agreements negotiated for high-capacity transit routes: granting fifty-year operating concessions to construct elevated automated sky-trains, backed by transparent inflation-indexed fare schedules submitted to the government.

## Project 'Giving Back'

As a software engineer at heart, I believe in beginning from version zero: you must start somewhere. Returning to Newton's maxim: standing on the shoulders of giants, we too must advance by building upon the accumulated wisdom of our predecessors. The best time to plant a tree was twenty years ago; if that did not happen, do we sit idly? No — we plant it today. Having served within the state agency that connected Bangladesh, my modest endeavor is to repay what this country invested in me. I have named this initiative Project 'Giving Back'. Think of it as an open-source blueprint for democratizing data literacy and broadband access. Drawing on the proven experiences of nations that mastered broadband transformation, I have sought to crack the code within our domestic context. Alongside this, national infrastructure funds and Special Purpose Vehicles (SPVs) will stand as essential pillars.

## In Search of the "Intelligent Systems" Project

Consider an advanced small nation: where purchasing property and securing certified ownership deeds takes a single week, our citizens endure months. To compress that turnaround, we must operate at scale — on a monumental level. To deliver public services to 160 million people, workflow automation is non-negotiable. While legacy software suffices today, it will inevitably collapse under tomorrow's demands. My core conviction is unequivocal: alongside traditional systems, intelligent systems must step forward to resolve societal inequities and bottlenecks. Intelligent systems will streamline governance, eliminate delays, and empower institutions. For instance, a unified national health card would provide public and private physicians with predictive diagnostics and longitudinal patient histories — directly serving citizens. We need intelligent systems across every facet of national life.
